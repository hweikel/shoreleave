No Friends but Mountains

2026 · 10 · 12

At 8:05pm in Oslo a sunset of such intensity is occurring over the harbor that a healthy minority of the hundreds assembled on the slopes of the opera house rooftop seem to be weeping, and yet all I can see in the shafts of flaming light that assault the city, glancing off the marble to fall extinguished in the harbor, or in the seagulls that stream in massive retaliatory formations straight back up toward God, are visions of the Shahed-136 kamikaze drone.

A wraith of a girl dances, eyes closed and hips tilting back and forth like a marionette, with a clairvoyant old Arab to the accordions shared by all of Old Europa. He's clairvoyant, I've decided after an hour of deliberation, because of the way he throws up both his palms and eyebrows in a hieratic gesture when anyone joins his improvised rooftop ballroom. Circling the dancers, I've started a fatalistic tally of the basic categories of human endeavor I've never reckoned with - and decided that among them dance is foremost; that it is beautiful; and that if I'm to die in a week anyways, I at least want to know what this one is called.

An elegant old woman lifts two enormous grey eyes from her folds of red-blue Hermes to tell me that it's called the Argentinian Tango - and warn me that I would do well to remember that fact. Then her daughter materializes beside us as if released from a bottle - hawklike, with makeup colored faintly like her mother's scarves - and promises that I could find this very dance being practiced at sunset in any city on earth.

They were the first of many people I'd meet over the coming weeks that I knew instinctively it would be unwise to name in writing. Their names weren't believable anyways - they had, like everything else on that rooftop, a filmic unreality that seemed to be settling back over my life like a storm from the East. They had once been Iraqis but were now simply rich - a coincidence that no longer surprised me as I confided desperately that I was on my way to Iraq, or Kurdistan, or whatever I should call it - that an inexplicable contract had arrived for me like a life raft, that I had taken it despite the counsel of everyone I knew, that I was in Oslo purely to kill a few days, looking for a serenity powerful enough to function as a protective coating - The mother cut me off and told me to dance with her daughter. The daughter looked at the horizon and demurred. Gratingly, I brought us back on topic: what awaited me in Erbil?

Then we settled into one another's company, forming a brief triangular communion on the marble benches, and I trembled as the accordions played and the mother conjured into the Oslo sunset images of a Sumerian city of concentric circles: Erbil, its innermost core the oldest fortification on earth, its outermost border a wall of olive trees being planted as we spoke - but all of it, every ring and district of the city, maintaining eight thousand years of complete circularity. These looping images presided over the coming days' series of buses and trains and flights, swirling me deeper and deeper in, until at the last moment escape was still possible, when I looked up at the flight's destination at Istanbul Airport and found myself on the lip of the whirlpool, I received a text from my employer that I was, God willing, to be picked up on the other side by a man named H—— who "knows every corner in this city" - a phrase that struck me for its ring of Borgesian truth, especially when said of concentric Erbil - a city with absolutely no corners at all.

God was willing, so we came in low over round huts, crop circles, craters blackened by mortar fire. H—— stepped out of blinding desert light to greet me in the airport lobby, then showed me to his beautiful German car, its body formed from some material like coagulated night that seemed inaccessible to the sun. H—— possessed the slumped nobility of a satyr: deep-set green eyes under piles of curls, the first hint of the Kurdish physiognomy, like some Celtic tribe discovered in long and weary exile. We drove to a hotel where tiny, finely-formed guards (like dolls with their glossy eyes, paint-black mustaches, and little red cheeks) ran out to greet us with Soviet AKs, the clips absurdly elongated like ornamental daggers, then clicked their heels and hoisted a gate. As soon as I opened my room's window, the call to prayer rang out over a city that looked like the average of two conflicting processes: half the buildings half-built, the others half-bombed away. Driving around over the next week, I found that it was often hard to tell the difference.

I needed a haircut, so H—— took me to NazNaz, a quarter composed of high, cave-mouth facades, deep into which were scattered little tables where groups of preened doll-men sat smoking and eating cake - a scene so far from the western imagination of Iraq that it makes our news coverage seem purposefully dishonest. He took me to an Iranian barber who raised my hair into a nest of pommade in a marble hall, whispering all the while promises that his young assistant translated, "I will do something to you that they haven't seen on Park Avenue". Then I walked with H—— and his friends - three sweet young Kurds on the cusp of marriage, who spoke between them six languages with complete comfort, moving between them as a form of subtle humor - to the whitewashed brick of a cafe. I was touched by the warmth of the evening - by how quick these men were to hoist me into hugs or roar with good humor - especially as they recounted, days earlier, watching from the rooftop cafes as thousands of rockets fell from the sky, or mimicked the sound of the the defensive volleys returned by the PATRIOT and C-RAM systems in the diplomatic outskirts a short walk away. They gave me a rapid history of the Kurdish people that culminated in the present impasse - a fragile semi-statehood achieved within Iraq, which, like the very moment of high tide, seemed already be falling away; they explained how all this connected to the fall of Saddam and those frantic end-of-everything nights as ISIS razed Mosul and came within twenty miles of Erbil; the jubilation of US airstrikes, like fire from heaven, routing the armies literally at the outermost gate of the concentric city. They pointed up at the American zeppelin hovering above us, a dim pale blob over the neon strip and named it TARS, the tethered aerostat radar system - explaining that it guided the PATRIOT system's interception of drones and missiles from hundreds of miles away; that for however long it bobbed serenely 750 meters over the city, we could drink coffee and laugh as the rockets appeared briefly on the horizon and were destroyed. (If you look up this fact now, you will find all sorts of falsehood and speculation: that the balloon was removed months ago, that it never existed at all. My time in Kurdistan emphasized how little wartime news could be trusted.)

Back in Oslo, the general counsel of a shipping firm had told me that it wasn't the explosive force but rather the fragments of glass from shattered windows that were most likely to kill you in a drone strikes. A worse death, too, he'd said: you bled out slowly. His strategy for a warzone hotel was to sleep as far away from windows as possible, ideally in the bathtub. Instead, as was perhaps inevitable, these kind men drove me back to my hotel and I fell asleep in bed with the curtains and windows thrown wide open to the light and noise of the city, my only concession to shrapnel a mountain of pillows heaped hastily between me and the sky.

I was in Erbil to train one group of people how to train another. If you were to pick two nouns at random from the hat of modernity, you'd likely get close to the themes I was improvising around as I attempted to fill three days - twice, first in Erbil, then in Sulimaniyah near the Iranian border.

At breakfast on the first day, I found my hotel nearly empty - "the long quiet season", the porter joked - populated only be a handful of Chinese engineers, a lone South African PMC, and a pair of Iraqi generals chainsmoking with little metal swords crossed over the shoulders of their black uniforms in place of epaulets. Equally smoky music played: the music of smoke-filled tents and barefoot dances, a lone meandering clarinet alternating with sequences that recalled great jaunty hunts at sea. I raised my coffee cup to my Chinese and Afrikaner counterparts out across the vast bright breakfast hall and was met, that morning and each to follow, with no response at all.

My handler Z—— picked me up in her own German car. She was tiny and shrewd, mixed Arab and Kurd, quick to laugh breathily in a way that made me trust her completely. The headquarters where we were to prepare sat in a row of identical white McMansions flanking some sun-baked palms out on the edge of the outer ring road. There I was introduced to M——, my orange-whiskered assistant, who immediately involved me in the magnetic physical comedy of the Upper East Side despite being, he assured me, entirely Kurdish and devoutly Muslim at that. Our cohort was completed when my employer S—— arrived - the first woman I'd met in years taller than I am, regal, in possession of glowing green-grey eyes mirrored by a pair of Van Cleef earrings that dangled out, the exact color and size, like the second set of eyes of a deep sea creature. She was almost too visually distinct to look at, as if animated in a different style, a central character taken from the wrong film. S—— told me, within minutes of our meeting, that one of her earliest memories was Saddam burying the people of her village alive (I couldn't comprehend what this meant at first, so she clarified: driven at gunpoint into sandy mass graves and then embedded in the desert) - that she had fled this scene on foot into the mountains, then to Iran while her father and grandfather stayed behind to rally and lead rebel bands before dying eye-to-eye with Saddam.

We broke from these revelations to lunch and then, with little discussion of the work I was to perform over the next ten days, I was back at my hotel, waiting for sunset. Already bored of NazNaz's glamour, I took a cab toward the innermost citadel, but we quickly ran into a roadblock manned by the army - who explained that it was the Prophet's birthday, and that meant I would have to continue on foot. I stepped out into a hellish scene: thick smoke from street fires split a maroon sky as barefoot workers attempted to pry up a uniform carpet of trampled plastic bottles. I followed spotlights and fireworks into the bazaar and then up to the great ringwall of the citadel, lifted up on a hillside of brightly lit dead grass. Dazed by bad air and heat and the beauty of the Kurdish families - walking hand in hand, some singing, through the smog - I began my long lonely loops around this inner sector. Quickly all light fell away except the stadium lamps, angled up at the dead grass of the embankment slope - illuminating families of yellow dogs that lunged and snapped through the fence.

Away from the bazaar, the streets were silent and bombed out, with facades gaping open like dislocated jaws. I decided to insert myself as deep into this environment as possible - off the main road, past destroyed huts and empty chicken coops and ransacked shops, back into the deepest silence of an alley, a place with no light but the full moon, and there I knew briefly the true distance of travel - how easy it was to step out of the "real world" and into the world of the newsreel, how nobody can stop you simply boarding a flight into the Level 4 Travel Advisory, and within less than a day you're surrounded by good, real people who will never be able to follow you back out - then all at once a young boy rounded the corner at a jog as if he had been watching from the shadows and planning this encounter, holding eye contact the whole time as he neared me and extended his hand mid-step, then silently shook my own in two polite little pumps and ran away. Restored by the sturdy manners of even this most distant back alley, I pushed on, walking in sleepless circles through that city of nested rings, past children limping through the night on bad legs, two brothers squabbling with the families of dogs over scraps, until I found another roadblock that marked the exit from the Prophet's birthday bash, and returned to the unearned luxury of my hotel.

One of the people I was training was named F——. We gathered each day in a faded red cube, centered within a compound of chainlink fences. F—— had black hair swept-back in a princely bob. I never once saw him blink. Every day during breaks in our training, he brought me black coffee from the staff lounge and asked me questions about Western esoteric literature and the Gnostic tradition, then told me about the archaeological record of our colonization from the stars. He was the student of a recently deceased German syncretist I hadn't heard of. He was preparing to embark on a serious engagement with Mann and Hesse, seeing already in their work all the hallmarks of alien influence. None of this had anything to do with his work, or what he was being trained to do. In all these traits he was, as one of the other trainees explained, simply an Iranian Kurd, modeling the admirable Kurdish tendency to hold numerous highly specific positive stereotypes about one another.

After the third day of training, F—— took me aside and whispered, wide-eyed, that I was invited to play for the American Christian Ultimate Frisbee Team of Erbil that night in a friendly match. He would pick me up at my hotel in two hours. He was not Christian, he assured me, nor American, he added with a sweet little laugh, but he found the sport a potent spiritual outlet. My complete inexperience was of no concern at all.

We played hard under a huge orange moon on a pitch of threadbare astroturf in Ankawa, the Christian quarter. The game was presided over by a missionary, one of those heroic old-millennial Americans who take everything in their life completely seriously, too busy excelling to notice anything unusual about running a faith-based frisbee league in a warzone. I was in a lineup beside a pair of Palestinian twins, recently fled to Erbil, who looked for all the world like Scots - dense freckles, orange hair - and afterward, panting and laughing with them about some triviality of the game, I thought of how we had destroyed these brothers' homes, their families and lives, in service of the Abrahamic death cult - and found absurd tears forming in my eyes. I tried to turn away and play it off, but one of the brothers noticed and took me by the shoulder and hugged me with a sweet patting of my rib cage, and laughed heartily, and then F—— appeared beside us, seemed to understand the situation in all of its layers at a glance, and took my other shoulder, gesturing up at the moon - "If you look back far enough in the record, we all came from the same place. No human religion or human state" (he colored each "human" with the weary irony of the conspiracist, then gave a little tinkling laugh) "can change that. We all have more in common than you fear, even those who have done worse than you."

Driving back to the hotel, our car broke down in the road and a few people swerved across eight lanes to give us a jump start. They too were immediately involved in the sphere of physical affection, comedy, and goodwill that seemed to radiate out from every person, from the city itself. Weeks on, F—— still sends me voicenotes, heedless of the city's deteriorating conditions, that narrate his rapid progress through the sacred texts, charting associations between The Magic Mountain, Rumi, ancient aliens. They all deserve so much more, the Kurds - and the strange random privilege - how vague a word until now! - of my crash-landing here brings me - like those moments when I imagine my parents sad or alone - straight to choked back, too-sudden sobs, like a misfiring sneeze.

I had been growing this whole time closer to my employers, who had started to feel more like a warm little family. We left Erbil the next day to drive out to Sulimaniyah, locally shortened to "Suli" in a way that made it feel all the more mystical, past military checkpoints where I was hugged by each guard and asked about the NBA, up into the very mountains that the Kurds had held as rebels. Idling on a narrow pass, as first one then six more eagles circled us, they told me stories from the rebel days, then we sat silently for a long time after H—— quoted a proverb: "We Kurds have no friends but mountains".

Suli was splayed out across the desert like an apocalyptic Denver. The second training took place in a newly developed compound set up into the foothills: the exact frontier of the city's progress out and upward, slopes sliced off at a neat line and bandaged with a thin retaining wall, into the shadow of which had been inserted schools and shops and a mimicry of American suburbs. The angles were such that while trying to lecture I would frequently catch a nauseating glimpse through a window across what seemed to be four or five incompatible planes of space: under a playground slide, over a cement wall, then across dead-gold hills layered with the over-simplified projections used in children's illustrations. Up there bulldozers were slicing off new hills, adding the foundations of villas-to-be, making the most of long-dormant oil money in this brief and fading period of stability.

My assistant M——, a Suli native, regarded all this development with energetic dismay. Surging around the new facility, he was always diving under desks to plug in an electric kettle and brew tea, or shouting at passersby to sit down with us and eat. If you ever stopped eating during a meal and glanced in his direction, he would yell "You're makin' me nervous!" or if some builders across the street paused in their work, he'd say "I can't eat with them watching - I can't do it", and soon they'd be sharing lunch beside us as well. After we finished the day's training M—— would take me to his childhood neighborhood, the bazaar. It was rich glittering chaos shaded by a dappled canopy. As we walked the warren of stalls that sloped down under the mosque, M—— would get so worked up that his tour-guide shpiel would digress and soar, "I'm telling you:" - he'd start, as if I'd disagreed - "This is my home. I grew up in the dirt and smells of this market. Even as a child I hated these stalls where they measure out live chicks with buckets." The vendor in question grinned up at us mid-pour, enjoying his role in the scene. M——'s hand was always on the shoulder of whomever he was speaking to, whomever he was speaking about. "I hate them still. But at least they have no pretense. All forms of cleanliness offend me." Moved by this speech - and in awe of how full and rich of a personality he commanded even in his fourth language - I sat with him to eat a meal of raw vegetables and suck down juice from a shared bucket.

That last night in Suli, sitting in the most celestial breeze in a back alley cafe as we contemplated my looming departure, the fever began. I regarded the scene as if from thirty feet overhead, watching as my dear employers laughed and schemed; the owner rasped to us in a wraithlike voice; the waiter passed me bone china thimbles of increasingly exotic coffees ("it involves the sap of that tree, right there, I assure you it is so") that we shotted, arms intertwined; the potted citrus trees swayed in the wake off passing cabs; a paper lantern swung half obscured by a drooping vine - and all the while I felt as if I were orbiting it all, floating up and slowly away.

On the drive back to Erbil, the Kurdish mountains presented their most Californian face, like driving down the Salinas Valley at dusk. By then my fever was such that I was lapsing briefly into incomprehensible speeches that made my little car-full of hosts, friends, & employers increasingly nervous. Once we arrived, I was medicated and entrusted to my old hotel, which I immediately left to walk through a vast ancient garden in cold sweat and delirium, around circular paths lit like Mr. Tumnus' glade, then returned to bed, circling death I thought - just as outside Iranian drones arrived to drag the city back into the Gulf Stream of history.

I woke some ten hours later to chaos, fixated in the fever by contemplation of the phrase "all hell broke loose". Detail was light, but the porters were driven to crazed eloquence in their little emerald uniforms in the hallway, debating like Shakespearean extras whether the American blimp had been pierced "like a javelin through a pig" or was "shot dead where it lay" in the American fortifications. Either way, TARS was gone and with it, the safety of the US military; now the buildings swayed like stalks of grass as PATRIOT interceptors fired and missed, and C-RAM lit the sky like those 2001 news reels of tracer rounds over Baghdad. Half a mile away, the American base was a column of smoke; footage played on the lobby TVs of soldiers crawling out of the rubble of a basement. My flight to Turkey - and all others - were grounded.

At dawn I was driven to our McMansion HQ, where talk of armored cars to Duhok, crossings of the Turkish land border, and envelopes of cash sketched paths out that were just as quickly erased as the phones rang and the walls around the region came down. Useless in my state, I thought dumbly of how thin the string is that holds us to the comfort of our reality - how far out that string can let you wander, so confident that it will pull you back at any moment with a quick tug - and how quickly it can be severed.

I thought of the hawklike apparition on her Oslo rooftop and was willing to swear that nobody in this whole city was dancing the Argentinian Tango now.

The last flight out, on which I had been secured a place by days of phone calls and what I could tell were the callings-in of multigenerational favors, pierced through tauntingly clear skies two mornings later.

Beside me, my neighbor Hend cast furtive glances out the window while telling me about her home, my new and completely unexpected destination: Qatar. She showed me on the monitor's remarkably HD map the contours of her apartment's balcony. Despite the calm skies, everyone on the flight looked to be clutching their seats. Flying in over Bahrain, Hend tugged on my shirt and said, "See that?" and I looked out to see warships - "The American fleet. If we go down, it is now." She laughed so that her little second chin shook. For the next twenty minutes she muttered what I took to be prayers. Then, in a moment of delirious jocularity when it was announced that we had cleared the Straight, I pulled the envelope of cash I had been given from its hiding place in my waistband, fanned the bills, and told her that I was experiencing some of the most distinct moments of my life. Hend considered all this for a while and then said with thoughtful precision, "My young friend, if it fits in that envelope, it will not be at risk in Doha."

At the border, Hend pulled me past the line right up to the citizens-only counter and announced, as if they would care, that I was her "guest in our country of Qatar". They did care, and within minutes we were pushing 110mph through a financial district that made a mockery of Riyadh, like a city composed fractally of other cities, each building enclosing others, contorting at moments even to enclose itself, then flaring out, vying with its neighbor to spiral up into a starless sky; so vast and coherent as to almost achieve taste, or at least to overpower the faculty of taste; the Final City, surely - pushing out of sandy shallows like the iridescent bristles of some great reclining boar.

I was deposited with all of Hend's blessings on an artificial island in the bay, where I walked through a hot night down rows of identical French brasseries. Men dressed like little steampunk bellhops served hot chocolate from brass basins they pushed on great wheeled carts, pausing between each ladlefull to ring a bell that dangled from the cart's striped awning. Every restaurant was patronized by an unending cohort of identical men - the same starched high-collared robe, the same mustache and perfume. I walked under a stone arch and out onto an abandoned shoreline promenade, where a Cambodian worker ran up to hand me a slice of honey cake encased in a wax paper sheath ("no payment, it is my pleasure") - then passed a beachside arena hosting a falcon hunting tournament (I saw only one bird for an instant as it reached its crest, turned its wings to catch the stadium lights, & dove) - and continued up a sloping grass hillside into a neighborhood enclosed behind a curtain wall of carved glowing topiary, at the base of which a dozen men dug a ditch, backlit against a massive villa - the rhythmic strikes and heaves of their shovels like an operatic rendering of a chain gang. I followed a path of little footlights down a path of baby clover (so small & finely formed & blessed with white flowers in that unreal, close-up light) and felt the whole time as if I were sliding through folds of hot air, lubricated by my own sweat, rather than walking.

I found a pier and followed it out past rows of yachts and cabanas to the massive granite doors of a restaurant. When I opened them after a long hesitation, an African woman, already waiting for me inches away in a green velvet suit, asked me if I would be smoking or not? - and I replied - sweating through my backpack, hair matted - by asking dumbly if they had a table for one? But she just smiled like a nurse or an angel and led me past dozens of booths where regal old Qataris smoked in low light as young women sat in crescent shapes around them and rubbed their hands - to a little table by a low glass wall so near the night sea that I could hear the waves lap at the edge of the floor tiles. The first item on the menu was a "full roast baby chicken" and I was so taken by the extravagant cruelty of that phrase that I ordered it before she left. She returned once to tear mint leaves into my cup of black tea and then again to deposit the chicken, tiny head intact and laid out like a funerary pyre in a ceramic ship.

When the check arrived in an indecipherable currency, I pulled a single hundred dollar bill from my waistband and handed it to the waitress, watching her face to see if this would be enough. When she held the same smile, I stood and motioned that I would leave, and she said that she hoped I enjoyed my time in the Middle East - and I told her, by then incubating the dysenteric filth that would rob me of 20 pounds over the coming week, already shaking with the dread of another flight over more contested water, and thinking of F—— and M—— and everyone I had left behind in Kurdistan, sealed in there like a crypt or a dream - that I had very much, but that I was ready to leave.

Two weeks later, all remaining US forces followed.
